Fisher Doubles Down

When Statistical Tools Support Prejudiced Beliefs

After World War II, the world declared, “never again.” Racial pseudoscience and the atrocities it justified faced universal condemnation. Yet, categorization itself persisted. Statistical methods advanced, but their application to groups of people continued to legitimize harmful hierarchies.

Ronald Fisher, a pioneer of modern statistics, carried the torch of eugenics from Francis Galton and Karl Pearson, both of whom laid the groundwork for applying statistical methods to human populations. Fisher’s contributions to statistics are immense: his work on analysis of variance (ANOVA), maximum likelihood estimation, and the design of experiments revolutionized mathematics, genetics, and scientific research. Yet, Fisher also believed deeply in eugenics, insisting that statistical tools could objectively determine which groups were "fit" to thrive. For Fisher, subjective judgments about "inferior" and "superior" populations became "scientific" through the application of mathematical methods.

Rather than abandoning eugenic thinking after the war, Fisher doubled down—arguing that the Earth’s finite resources required humanity to make hard choices. He claimed that statistical tools could help identify which populations contributed to sustainability and which were burdens. This rationalization gave his worldview a veneer of scientific legitimacy, even as it concealed its subjective roots.

Imagine a world where the survival of humanity hinges on resource scarcity. This was Ronald Fisher's rationale in the aftermath of World War II. He argued that statistical tools could determine which populations were most "valuable" to the planet’s sustainability and which were burdens. In his view, this logic demanded difficult decisions—up to and including the elimination of "inferior" populations. Statistical correlations became tools for moral and existential judgments.

Step into Fisher’s mindset. Pretend that the fate of the planet depends on your ability to categorize populations. Adjust the weights assigned to variables like IQ, moral character, and crime statistics to explore how hierarchies might emerge. To begin:

  • Click on the variable names at the top of the visualization to isolate or include them in the analysis. Consider which variables Fisher might have emphasized and why.
  • Use the sliders to assign weights to the variables. Higher weights amplify the influence of that variable on the bar chart. Lower weights diminish it.
  • Observe the results: How do your adjustments affect the ranking of groups? What narratives emerge from the data, and what assumptions do they reflect?

For instance, suppose that, like Fisher, you believed that the white "race" is superior to the black "race." With all the weights set to 1, the white group has a total score of 298.5, which is calculated by adding IQ, moral character, income, reproduction rate, and subtracting crime statistics. The black group, by comparison, scores 266. The scores are relatively close.

If you wanted to “prove” the superiority of the white group, you could adjust the weights for moral character and income to 2, amplifying their importance. This would result in a new total score of 446 for the white group and 375.5 for the black group.

But suppose, like Fisher, you viewed the higher reproduction rate among black populations as a problem for Earth's sustainability. By assigning a weight of 4 to reproduction rate and subtracting it (along with crime statistics) from the other variables, the scores shift dramatically: the white group would have 220.9, and the black group would fall to 117. This manipulation “proves” a narrative of inferiority based entirely on subjective choices about what matters.

Keep in mind that this data is randomly generated, but the exercise demonstrates how easily statistical tools can create narratives that reinforce preconceived beliefs.

While the overtly biased metrics of Fisher's time have faded, the mechanisms remain. Today, variables like educational attainment, health outcomes, and income disparity are used to measure societal progress. These metrics may seem impartial, but they are often laden with the values of those who define them.

This visualization invites you to engage with modern variables that are often used in discussions of equity and progress. Here’s how you can explore:

  • Click on the variable names at the top of the visualization to include or exclude them from the analysis. Think about which variables are prioritized in contemporary narratives of "success."
  • Adjust the sliders to assign weights to each variable. Observe how changing these weights shifts the bar chart and alters the perceived rankings of groups.
  • Reflect on the narratives created by your adjustments. Which groups appear to "succeed" under certain weightings? Which are marginalized? How does this depend on the values embedded in the variables?

This data is also randomly generated, but it highlights the enduring power of statistical tools to shape narratives. Whether through Fisher’s eugenicist lens or today’s metrics of progress, the ability to assign weights reveals the subjective nature of these tools.

These exercises reveal how statistical tools, designed to understand the world, can easily divide it. From Fisher’s justification of resource allocation to today’s efforts to measure societal progress, the ability to assign weights and create hierarchies reflects subjective values rather than objective truths.

Fisher’s methods reveal the inherent subjectivity of statistical tools. By assigning weights and defining variables, these tools do more than describe reality—they prescribe values and impose hierarchies. Whether justifying resource allocation in Fisher’s era or defining societal progress today, statistical frameworks reflect the biases of those who create them. They are not neutral; when applied to human populations, they become instruments of exclusion.

The persistence of IQ as a central metric exemplifies this issue. While IQ measures something quantifiable, it remains deeply entwined with assumptions about intelligence and human worth. Fisher’s belief in its importance, like his focus on other traits, was shaped by a worldview that sought to categorize and control. Even today, IQ’s prominence in debates about education, employment, and societal potential reflects these enduring biases
