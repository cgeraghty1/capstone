Fisher's Perspective on Population and Sustainability

After the devastation of the Second World War, the world resolved to rebuild, declaring "Never again." Amid this resolve, R.A. Fisher, one of the most influential statisticians of the 20th century, doubled down on his controversial beliefs. Fisher’s groundbreaking contributions to statistics—such as analysis of variance (ANOVA), maximum likelihood estimation, and experimental design—transformed genetics, biology, and social sciences. Yet, these methods also became tools in the service of eugenicist ideology.

Fisher, believed that statistical tools could be applied to identify and prioritize "fit" populations for the greater good of humanity and the Earth. He argued that encouraging the reproduction of groups he deemed genetically "superior" was essential to ensuring humanity’s long-term sustainability and avoiding the depletion of resources. Fisher saw this as a moral and scientific imperative, one aligned with maintaining the Earth’s balance.

“The practical international problem is that of learning to share the resources of this planet amicably with persons of materially different nature, and that this problem is being obscured by entirely well-intentioned efforts to minimize the real differences that exist.”

This logic, though framed as scientific, reveals how powerful statistical tools can be shaped by the subjective biases of their creators. While such methods are invaluable for understanding complex phenomena, they can also obscure their subjective roots when applied to categorizing and judging populations.

An Experiment in Subjective Weighting

Below, you will find an interactive tool designed to explore how subjective weights influence outcomes. The industries represented—Big Pharma, Oil Industry, Tech Industry, Industrial Agriculture, and Luxury Goods—are often perceived as contributing to global challenges in distinct ways. Consider why someone might criticize each:

  • Big Pharma: High drug prices and perceived exploitation of vulnerable populations.
  • Oil Industry: Environmental degradation and resistance to renewable energy initiatives.
  • Tech Industry: Data privacy concerns and monopolistic practices.
  • Industrial Agriculture: Environmental damage and poor labor conditions.
  • Luxury Goods: Wasteful production practices and reliance on sweatshop labor.

Adjust the sliders to assign weights to these variables. Observe how the bar chart to the left dynamically updates, reflecting the weighted values for each industry. Below the chart, a horizontal bar chart shows the total scores for each industry, highlighting which one appears to be the most problematic based on your subjective inputs.

The exercise demonstrates how subjective choices can manipulate outcomes—even when backed by complex mathematical models. The same logic and statistical reasoning that once categorized races can be applied today to industries or other groups, shaping narratives to fit preconceived notions. As you explore, ask yourself: Are the results reflecting reality, or merely your own perspective?

The methodology of assigning subjective weights to variables is not confined to the past. Today, these principles are applied in contemporary frameworks like the World Happiness Report, where well-being is measured through carefully chosen variables. This raises critical questions: Do these measures genuinely reflect happiness, or do they mirror the priorities and biases of their creators?

The World Happiness Report offers a case study in how subjective weights shape perceptions of well-being. As we delve into this modern application, consider how these measures influence global narratives about happiness, often privileging certain values over others.
