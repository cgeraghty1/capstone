Introduction

Why do statistical tools so often fail to capture the unpredictable essence of human behavior? Election outcomes defy expectations, leaving analysts grappling with the question: why do entire demographics, seemingly bound by patterns, break from predicted narratives? How do the metrics we use to define happiness, intelligence, and success shape the systems that govern public policy and artificial intelligence? At their core, these tools—regardless of sophistication—rely on methods that reduce human complexity to patterns and predictions.

Data analysis thrives on patterns, but its reliance on averages and aggregates sacrifices nuance. Outliers are dismissed as exceptions, and unique experiences are flattened into statistics. Visualization, while celebrated for its clarity, can transform data into compelling yet reductive stories—narratives that shape public perception and policy. While these tools wield undeniable power, their unexamined biases perpetuate narratives that fail to fully represent human diversity.

Consider the World Happiness Report, which ranks nations based on select metrics. Its conclusions suggest clarity, but do such metrics truly capture the profound and deeply subjective nature of happiness? Election forecasts and demographic analyses attempt to predict behavior but falter in the face of individual complexity. These tools reveal less about the people they categorize and more about the philosophical and cultural biases of their creators.

This project examines the roots of these tools, focusing on the work of figures like Francis Galton, Karl Pearson, and Ronald Fisher. Their statistical innovations were used to justify biases under the guise of objectivity. By embedding subjective values into metrics, they transformed personal judgments into systems that categorized and ranked human lives. This project critiques these frameworks and their enduring influence, urging us to question what is lost when humanity is reduced to numbers.
