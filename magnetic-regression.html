Scatterplots and the Magnetic Pull of the Regression Line

Regression analysis identifies trends in data by drawing a line that captures the central relationship between variables. This line exerts a subtle pull, organizing data around the average in what could be called a “magnetic regression.” Each data point is drawn toward this line, as if conforming to an imposed ideal. While this process simplifies complexity and reveals patterns, it also reframes variability as deviation—a characteristic to be corrected or contained.

The visualization above represents Galton’s data on parent and child heights, reimagined as a scatterplots: a field of points clustering near the regression line, with others scattered farther afield. This data, within its context, is scientifically valid—it accurately measures the relationship between heights. But the framework through which such data is interpreted carries far deeper implications. Immanuel Kant’s philosophy, particularly his concept of a priori categories—mental frameworks that structure perception—provides a structure for understanding the world that assumes certain mental frameworks are universally valid. While these categories are necessary for structuring perception, they also invite subjectivity, especially when applied by those in positions of power. If one accepts Kant’s framework, elites like Francis Galton and Karl Pearson could rationalize their own hierarchical categorizations of human groups as not only correct but scientifically justified.

In this way, Galton’s work—and later Karl Pearson’s mathematical innovations—transformed subjective ideas about order and hierarchy into seemingly objective tools. Pearson’s statistical methods, like regression analysis, gave the appearance of neutrality, allowing subjective categorizations to be expressed in the language of science. The "pull" of the regression line symbolizes this transformation: a way of reshaping not just data but societal understanding around the priorities and assumptions of those who wield these tools. What began as cultural or personal biases about human worth became encoded into scientific models, further legitimizing the belief that deviations from the norm were deficiencies to be corrected.

Outliers—those points that resist the pull of the line—represent a challenge to this imposed order. Yet their exclusion from deeper inquiry reinforces the idea that difference equates to deficiency, erasing the complexity and richness of human experience. The challenge is not in questioning the validity of the data itself but in interrogating the frameworks used to interpret it, and the subjective values that underpin those frameworks.
