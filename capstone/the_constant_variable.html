The Constant Variable: IQ

A Critical Examination of IQ in Statistical Analysis

We stand at a pivotal moment in the history of intelligence. For centuries, humanity has sought to measure and rank intellectual capacity, often reducing its complexity to a single score: IQ. Today, artificial intelligence—a form of intelligence existing alongside us rather than within us—challenges our understanding of intelligence itself. AI’s rise compels us to confront a fundamental question: Why have we sought to rank people by intelligence, and how might we break free from this enduring compulsion?

The statistical tools that power AI—linear regression, probability theory, and neural networks—are rooted in methods developed by figures like Francis Galton, Karl Pearson, and Ronald Fisher. These pioneers sought to distill human complexity into patterns and predictions, using metrics like IQ to categorize individuals and rank groups. AI has inherited this mathematical legacy. While these frameworks have enabled extraordinary technological advances, they also carry forward the biases of their creators. The same statistical principles once used to justify hierarchies of intelligence and human worth now influence AI systems, raising profound ethical concerns.

As other metrics evolved to reflect new societal priorities, IQ persisted, shaping narratives about human worth for over a century. From its origins in Galton’s obsession with measuring traits to its use in Pearson’s correlation models and Fisher’s statistical advancements, IQ has been a cornerstone of comparative analysis. Yet, its dominance reflects the priorities of those who devised it—priorities steeped in subjectivity, hierarchy, and exclusion.

In the early 20th century, IQ became a tool of policy and control. Immigration quotas in the United States were shaped by intelligence tests that disproportionately excluded non-European populations. The U.S. Army ranked recruits by IQ during World War I, embedding these metrics into institutional decision-making. Fisher, extending these ideas, argued that intelligence scores could guide reproduction policies to ensure global sustainability. Intelligence became a metric of profound societal consequence—a weaponized variable wielded to justify exclusion and control.

But IQ, as a measure, is deeply flawed. Its benchmarks were defined by the cultural and intellectual norms of its creators, embedding their biases into the scoring system. The result was not an objective measure of intelligence but a reflection of societal values that prioritized some traits while marginalizing others. Even today, the persistence of IQ as a metric says more about humanity’s obsession with ranking than about intelligence itself.

Artificial intelligence offers an opportunity to transcend this legacy—but only if we approach its design with caution. Unlike humans, AI lacks consciousness, creativity, and self-awareness. Its intelligence lies in processing data and executing tasks—abilities shaped entirely by the data it is trained on and the frameworks that underlie its programming. If AI systems are trained on biased datasets or imbued with the same categorical thinking that underpins IQ, they risk perpetuating the same hierarchies they have the potential to dismantle.

Consider how AI systems already reinforce biases. Social media platforms like Facebook and TikTok prioritize content based on engagement metrics such as clicks, shares, and time spent on a post. If polarizing content generates more interaction, the AI amplifies it, reinforcing division and conflict. The variables prioritized—designed to serve the platform’s goals—often reflect commercial interests rather than societal well-being. This mirrors the subjectivity of historical metrics like IQ: What is measured, and why, determines the narratives these systems promote.

Now imagine an AI system designed to recommend policies for "maximizing happiness," based on metrics from the World Happiness Report. This report places significant weight on variables like GDP and social support, with social support measured through a single survey question: “If you were in trouble, do you have relatives or friends you can count on to help you whenever you need them?” This variable, while compelling, is inherently subjective and culturally specific. An AI trained on such data might propose policies that disproportionately benefit societies aligning with this metric while marginalizing others with different cultural frameworks for well-being. This approach risks creating a prescribed model of happiness, reflecting the priorities of those who define the metrics rather than the diversity of human experience.

The implications are profound. From IQ to happiness scores, the act of assigning weights and defining categories reflects the biases and values of those in power. These systems, often presented as objective, continue to impose narratives that marginalize those who do not fit predefined molds. AI, while offering extraordinary potential, carries the same risks if its development is not guided by ethical scrutiny and an understanding of history.

At its best, AI can redefine intelligence as collaborative, adaptive, and inclusive, moving beyond rigid metrics like IQ. It can become a tool for expanding our understanding of intelligence rather than reinforcing existing hierarchies. But this depends on the choices we make now. Will we continue to assign subjective weights to variables, embedding inequities into the systems we create? Or will we use AI to embrace complexity, diversity, and the richness of human potential?
